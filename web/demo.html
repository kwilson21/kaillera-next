Rollback netplay runs your game locally without waiting on the network. It predicts what the opponent will press, and rewinds-and-replays when it guesses wrong — invisible at low latency, visible as judder at high latency. Either way your input still lands at the current frame, which is the point: rollback hides the network from your hands, not necessarily from your eyes. Lockstep, the older approach, waits for the opponent's input every frame, so every keypress carries the round-trip delay directly. The round-trip is on the wire either way; the two modes just choose where to pay the cost.

The proof is the contrast: at the same simulated RTT, rollback keeps your input instant while lockstep makes every press arrive late. Lockstep stays smoother visually (it never rewinds); rollback's framerate dips at high RTT because predictions miss more often and the engine has more state to replay. The rollback button toggles between them; auto-compare flips every 6 seconds so you can feel the difference without clicking.

In this demo P2 is a synthetic peer running in the same tab. During menus it follows a baked autopilot; in match it plays random inputs at roughly human cadence. Each time the peer's input differs from what the engine predicted, the engine rewinds — that fills the Rollbacks counter and (at high RTT) shows up in the fps readout. Crank the RTT slider higher and the count rises: that's what rollback is hiding from your inputs but not from your eyes.
